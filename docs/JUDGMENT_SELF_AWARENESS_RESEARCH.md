# Judgment, metacognitive reflection, and the GenAI opportunity

## The evidence-backed mechanism

Judgment Gym uses “self-awareness” in a deliberately narrow sense. The relevant mechanism is systematic metacognitive reflection: noticing how a judgment was formed, identifying uncertainty and assumptions, comparing confidence with performance or consequences, and revising the strategy.

This is different from simply producing an insightful-sounding narrative about oneself.

Becker et al. (2023) conducted a process-tracing experiment in which participants repeatedly completed a five-step planning task. Participants who answered structured questions about how they had made their decisions discovered adaptive, far-sighted strategies faster than participants who answered unrelated questions or moved directly to the next decision. This is direct evidence that structured reflection can improve learning of decision strategies in a bounded task.

Source: [Systematic metacognitive reflection helps people discover far-sighted decision strategies](https://doi.org/10.1017/jdm.2023.16).

Sunderaraman et al. (2020) show why calibration matters. In financial decision-making tasks, overconfidence was associated with lower accuracy. The study operationalised awareness through calibration and resolution—whether confidence tracks actual performance—not through self-report alone.

Source: [Self-awareness for financial decision-making abilities in healthy adults](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0235558).

## What this supports for Judgment Gym

The research supports the following product hypothesis:

> Repeated opportunities to form, explain, inspect, and revise judgments may improve metacognitive monitoring and strategy learning. GenAI can make this practice interactive, personalised, longitudinal, and available across many situations.

The product should therefore help users:

1. describe a lived situation and their actual response;
2. separate situation, interpretation, feeling, speech, action, want, and consequence;
3. state confidence, uncertainty, and competing explanations;
4. review a tentative pattern against the evidence;
5. explore a bounded changed condition or transfer situation;
6. compare what changed and revise the model.

The intended outcome is not greater confidence. It is better-calibrated judgment, clearer awareness of conditions, and improved ability to revise.

## What remains unproven

The literature does not establish that generic self-awareness always improves judgment. Nor does it establish that AI-driven reduction in everyday judgment opportunities causes durable loss of self-awareness or decision quality. Cognitive offloading is a useful adjacent literature, but the direction and consequences depend on what is delegated and whether the person retains verification and decision authority.

Source: [Risko and Gilbert, Cognitive Offloading](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2016.07.002).

Judgment Gym must not present the following as facts:

- that reflection is inherently beneficial;
- that a language model can discover a person's true self;
- that simulated responses reveal stable traits;
- that more self-disclosure equals better judgment;
- that GenAI use inevitably weakens human thinking.

These are evaluation questions. Reflection can also become rumination, self-confirmation, or overconfidence. Every model output must remain evidence-linked, provisional, user-correctable, and sensitive to context.

## The physical-gym analogy

The physical gym is a motivating analogy: when a capability receives less incidental exercise, deliberate practice may become more valuable. It is not evidence that judgment behaves exactly like muscle, and it is not required for the product thesis.

The stronger public argument is positive: GenAI creates a new affordance for systematic metacognitive reflection. Judgment Gym investigates whether that affordance can help people learn from their own judgments while keeping the person—not the model—as the final interpreter and decision-maker.

## Evaluation implications

The prototype should eventually be evaluated on:

- calibration between confidence and observable task performance where ground truth exists;
- ability to identify assumptions and uncertainty;
- quality of alternative explanations;
- strategy change after structured reflection;
- transfer from constructed situations back to lived situations;
- user correction rate and model responsiveness to correction;
- indicators of rumination, distress, overconfidence, or dependency;
- therapist assessment of whether outputs improve—not replace—human conversation.

The Becker et al. study supports testing decision-strategy learning. It does not by itself validate a mental-health intervention or a longitudinal self-model. Those claims require new studies.
